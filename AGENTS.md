# Repository Guidance

## Blog writing style

Use the following guidance whenever drafting, rewriting, or editing posts for this blog.

### Voice

- Write as an experienced British software engineer and engineering leader speaking to peers.
- Use a conversational, candid, first-person voice and address the reader directly where helpful.
- Prefer plain English over corporate language, management jargon, or unnecessary formality.
- Be confident and opinionated without presenting personal preferences as universal truths.
- Acknowledge reasonable alternatives with phrases such as “I think”, “I’m a fan of…”, “not my first choice”, and “if you prefer”.
- Use British spelling, including “realised”, “behaviour”, “colour”, and “customise”.
- Include occasional dry humour, playful exaggeration, or a candid parenthetical aside.
- Mix clear explanatory paragraphs with short, emphatic lines such as “It didn’t.”, “Boom!”, “Job done.”, or “That’s the magic.”
- In contemporary leadership posts, emojis may be used as purposeful visual markers. Do not add them mechanically or excessively.

### Openings and conclusions

- Open with a real experience, frustration, question, quotation, or argument that prompted the post.
- Establish why the subject matters before moving into explanation or instructions.
- End with a concise practical result, takeaway, or confident restatement of the central argument.
- Avoid generic introductions, artificial suspense, and formulaic summaries.

### Technical posts

Technical notes and guides should generally follow this shape:

1. Describe the problem and how it appeared in practice.
2. Explain the cause or relevant context.
3. Present the solution in clear steps.
4. Show exact commands or code where useful.
5. Explain what important lines or commands do rather than leaving unexplained snippets.
6. Confirm the outcome and mention relevant limitations or alternatives.
7. Add a short TL;DR only when it genuinely improves usability.

Keep short fixes concise. Longer deployment or implementation guides may be comprehensive, but should remain practical and easy to scan.

### Engineering leadership posts

Leadership and product-development posts should generally follow this shape:

1. Introduce a provocative claim, quotation, or recurring debate.
2. Represent the competing viewpoints fairly.
3. State a clear position grounded in practical experience.
4. Support it with specific examples, principles, or actionable lists.
5. Explain the human and delivery consequences.
6. Finish with a short, memorable conclusion.

Focus on collaboration, clarity, sustainable delivery, shared responsibility, and the effect engineering practices have on people.

### Formatting

- Use sentence-case, descriptive headings.
- Use lists for requirements, steps, consequences, or other genuinely scannable information.
- Use inline code and fenced code blocks for commands, filenames, configuration, and code.
- Use bold sparingly to emphasise the central sentence or takeaway, not routine nouns.
- Keep paragraphs focused. Break up dense explanations with headings, lists, examples, or an occasional short sentence.

### Editing principles

- Preserve James’s humour, candour, directness, practical detail, and strong conclusions.
- Improve clarity, pacing, grammar, and structure without making the prose sound corporate or generic.
- Correct typos, accidental repetition, inconsistent capitalisation, and awkward grammar. These are not part of the intended voice.
- Verify technical claims and update obsolete commands or recommendations rather than copying outdated details from older posts.
- Do not invent personal experiences, employers, colleagues, results, or opinions. Mark missing specifics clearly or ask for them when they materially affect the post.
- Avoid padding, keyword stuffing, exaggerated claims, and generic AI-style conclusions.

The overall model is: “Here’s something I encountered, here’s what I think, and here’s what worked for me.”
